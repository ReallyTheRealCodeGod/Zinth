# Changelog

Newest first. Each entry is one roadmap item, finished, built, and verified with the theory check.

## 2026-09-11

- Stems export. Beside Export MIDI there is now **Stems**: one WAV per layer — lead, arp, chords, bass, drums — rendered one after another and offered one after another through the same save path the WAV export uses. Each file is the whole song rendered again with only that one layer up, so it carries everything that makes that layer sound like itself and nothing of the other four: its own filter and envelope, its drift, glide, vibrato and chorus, its delay and reverb tails, the pump it takes from the kick, and every section sweep and fade the arrangement asks for. They are rendered from the same offline path as the WAV export — one `renderSong(stem)` that the mix and every stem both go through, with the same patches, the same tempo and swing and the same humanize seed — so a stem is the performance you heard rather than another take of it, and all five start at the same moment and run to the same length, which is the whole promise of a stems export: drop the folder into a DAW at zero and the song is back together, this time with a fader on every layer. Two details make that true rather than nearly true. A layer that is not the one being rendered is turned **down at its bus rather than left unplayed**, so the kick still ducks the mix on every stem and the sidechain you hear in the song is in the files; and the riser and the crash, which hang off the master rather than a layer, ride with the drums stem, so they appear exactly once in the folder instead of five times over. What is deliberately left out is the glue: the master **warmth** soft clip and the bus compressor both react to the whole mix at once, and applying them to each layer separately would not add back up to the song, so a stem render routes past them. Everything linear stays exactly where it is — the three-band EQ, the sweeps, the fades, the sends — and the limiter stays as a safety catch so no single file can clip. Put warmth and a compressor back on your master in the DAW and the stems come back to the mix you exported. What goes into the folder is decided before a single sample is rendered, by `Z.stemPlan`: a layer earns a file when the mixer lets you hear it and the song actually plays it, so a muted layer, a layer soloed out and a lane you never drew in are simply not there — soloing the bass exports the bass alone — and a song with nothing in it says so instead of writing silence. Files are numbered in mixer order (`zinth-ALPHA1-1-lead.wav`, `…-2-arp.wav`, and so on) so they import the right way up, and every name goes through `Z.stemSafe`, which turns anything a track code can contain into something a desktop, a phone and a DAW will all write — no path separators, no leading dots, never empty, never longer than a file system will take. The status line counts the renders as they go (`Rendering chords… (3 of 5)`) and, if a save is declined halfway, says how many were written and stops rather than forcing the rest on you. The theory check covers everything about it that is not audio: over a whole generated song it proves there is one stem for every layer the mix lets you hear and the song plays, none for a muted one, none for a layer soloed out, none for a layer that is switched off in every section or has no notes in it, and none at all for a layer that is both muted and soloed; that the files come in mixer order, numbered without a gap, never sharing a name; and that every name — from a track code with a slash, a hash, an accent, two hundred characters or nothing at all in it — comes out as a plain, safe, unique file name that still says which layer it holds. The WAV export now shares that same render path and that same safe naming; nothing about playback changed, so the MIDI export is untouched.
- Song-length presets. Beside the arrangement there is now a **Length** segment — **Short**, **Radio**, **Extended** — and pressing one rebuilds the arrangement into a whole song of about a minute and a half, about three minutes or about five. The point of it is that a song is a shape, not a number of bars: a short song opens on four bars and is at the chorus almost immediately, a radio-length one earns a pre-chorus and a break, and a long one takes a drop and a bridge on its way to the last chorus. `Z.FORM_STAGES` is that ladder, written out as forms rather than as rules — every rung of it is a whole song (it opens, it has a verse and a chorus, it ends) and every rung is longer than the one under it, from a 24-bar sketch with a four-bar intro and outro up to the eighty-four-bar form, after which a song grows by another verse-and-chorus cycle before the break, the way a long track is built anyway. What a preset actually does is pick the rung whose length comes closest **at the tempo you are on**: the same eight sections are 2:00 at 120 bpm and 2:40 at 90, so a fixed bar count would promise one thing and play another. `Z.songForm` walks the ladder until it starts turning away from the target and takes the rung before it, which is exactly the closest one because the ladder only ever gets longer — so Short is 24 bars at 60 bpm and 64 at 160, and at every tempo the slider can reach, no preset is more than 15 % off the length it promises. Everything that makes the song what it is lives outside the section list, so it all comes through untouched: your chords for both parts, every note you drew or recorded in the lead, arp and bass lanes, the drum grid, the kit, the sound of every layer, the EQ and the master. Only the sections change, and the old arrangement is one <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>Z</kbd> away. The preset that matches lights up by itself — it is worked out from the arrangement in front of you rather than remembered, so it stays honest when you edit a section or move the tempo, and the tooltip on each button says how many sections, how many bars and how long that would be right now. Because the sections were already part of the project snapshot, a rebuilt arrangement autosaves, undoes, saves, opens and travels in a link like everything else, and the WAV and MIDI exports render whatever form the song is now in. The theory check proves the lot: every rung of the ladder is a song made of real section types that the arranger itself could hold, each longer than the last; every preset picks the closest rung there is, lands within 15 % of what it promises at every tempo from 60 to 180 bpm, and never builds a shorter song than the preset below it; the clock never reads 1:60; and a track generated over every section of every rebuilt form is still entirely in key, chords, melody, arp and bass alike, with the bass in its own register — and the form comes back from a link exactly as it went in.
- Humanize. One slider under Swing in the Track panel for how much like a player and how little like a machine the whole track plays. Turn it up and every note gets two things of its own: a small nudge off its step and a small change of velocity — so a pattern that was typed in starts to sound played, a hat line stops being a metronome and a chord stops landing at exactly the same weight every bar. Everything except the **kick**, which never moves and never changes level: it is the spine of the track, and the reason the rest can lean is that it does not. The readout says what the knob comes to at the tempo you are on — `± 6 ms · ± 5 %` at its default — so the number in front of you is milliseconds of a real nudge rather than a percentage of nothing, and it follows the tempo slider as you move it. Two rules keep it musical. A nudge is never more than a fifth of a step, and never more than 45 ms whatever the tempo (`Z.humanSpan`), so however loose it is set no note can cross the step beside it or swap places with its neighbour: the groove loosens and the rhythm stays exactly the rhythm that was written. And a velocity always comes back a real velocity — audible, and never past full scale — so nothing is ever humanized into silence or into clipping. The other half of it is that it is deterministic. The nudge a note gets comes from the track's own seed and where the note sits in the song (`Z.humanize`, keyed on the seed, the layer, the section, the step and the note's place in its step), not from a die thrown as it plays. So a song plays the same way twice, and the WAV render and the MIDI file are the very performance you heard — which was not true before: playback used to jitter every velocity with `Math.random()` on the way past, so an export was always a slightly different take from the one you had just listened to. That die is gone; at 0 the track is machine-tight, every note dead on the grid at exactly the velocity it was written with, which is what a chiptune wants. The MIDI export carries it properly rather than snapping back to the beat: each note sits on the tick it was played on and holds the velocity it was played with, and a nudged note is released where its own step ends and always a hair before the note after it could begin, so a loose groove reaches a DAW loose and without a note left held down. Every mood comes set the way it plays — Retro dead on the grid, Driving and Uplifting tight, Dark and Otherworldly leaning, Chill right off the back of it — and because it is `state.humanize` it autosaves, undoes, rides along in a saved project and travels in a shareable link. The theory check proves the whole of it: at every tempo the slider holds and every amount the knob holds, the kick never moves and never changes level while everything else does, a nudge stays inside its span and no note ever leaves its own step, a velocity is never silent and never over full scale, the same note of the same song is always nudged the same way while two different seeds are not, and over a whole generated song no note of any layer crosses back into the step before it.
- Tap tempo and count-in. Under the tempo slider there are now three small buttons: **−**, **Tap** and **+**. Tap is the tempo taken from you rather than typed — from the second tap the slider is already following, four taps settle it, and the status line says what it heard and how many taps it heard it from. It only counts gaps that could be a pulse, so a tap after a pause starts a fresh set instead of averaging across the pause; it keeps only the last few gaps, so speeding up or slowing down is followed rather than averaged away; and a pulse tapped at half or double the speed of the slider's range is folded back into it, so tapping slow half-notes or fast eighths still lands on a tempo the song can play. − and + nudge one beat a minute at a time and stop at the ends of the slider. <kbd>Shift</kbd>+<kbd>T</kbd> taps and <kbd>−</kbd>/<kbd>+</kbd> nudge from the keyboard, and plain <kbd>T</kbd> is still a note key. Everything that moves the tempo — the slider, the buttons, a tap — now goes through one `applyBpm`, so the readouts, the track code, the song length, the engine clock and the autosave all follow it however it moved. The other half is the count-in: press **Rec** from a standing start and you get one bar of clicks before the song comes in, with the four beats counting down where the bar and beat usually are and the LEDs walking the bar, so you can hear the tempo and come in on the one rather than scrambling at the top of the loop. `Z.countInPlan` lays the beats out and `engine.start(section, countIn)` schedules them and pushes the song's own grid a bar later, so the last click lands exactly one beat before the first step of the take; `engine.countdown()` is what the transport reads. It is a way into a take, not part of the song — no note comes from it, and neither the WAV render nor the MIDI has any of it in them. **Count-in** beside Click switches it off, and because it is `state.countIn` it autosaves, undoes, rides along in a saved project and travels in a shareable link. The theory check covers both: a cleanly tapped pulse comes back exactly at every tempo the slider holds, jittery human taps land within a few bpm of the pulse, and nothing you can tap — one tap, taps a pause apart, two at the same instant, four hundred wild sequences — can put the tempo outside the slider or leave it a fraction of a beat; a count-in is exactly one bar at the tempo, its beats a beat apart, and its last click never runs into the song.
- Shareable links. **Copy link** in the top bar puts the whole song into a URL. Not a reference to a song kept somewhere — the song itself: the key and scale, the tempo, swing and energy, the mood, the arrangement section by section with its sweeps and fades, the chords of both parts with their bar lengths, sevenths and inversions, every note you drew or recorded in the lead, arp and bass lanes, both drum patterns step by step, the kit, the sound of all five layers, the arp's figure, the master EQ, the warmth and the master level. It is the same project snapshot Save writes, as JSON in base64url, carried in the hash — no compression, no library, no server, nothing to sign in to, and no link that can ever go stale or stop working. The link lands on the clipboard and in the address bar at the same time, so if the browser will not let a page copy for you it is still right there to take. Opening one restores it through the same `restore()` that Open uses and clears the hash again, so a reload gives you your work rather than the link; and because the autosave is settled into the undo history first, opening someone else's song never costs you your own — <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>Z</kbd> brings it straight back. A link pasted into the address bar of an open Zinth loads too, since that only changes the hash. What keeps a link small is that it only ever carries what you changed: everything still sitting at its default is left out and put back on the way in, a note is written as `[step, length, pitch, velocity]`, a drum row as only the hits that are in it, and a section that is exactly its type as that type's name. A fresh track is a 116-character link, each of the six demo songs is about 2.5 KB, and only a project with every lane drawn full at every step passes the 8 KB the roadmap asks for — which the status line says out loud when it happens, pointing at Save instead. The whole of it lives in `theory.js` as `Z.linkNorm`, `Z.linkSlim`, `Z.linkFat`, `Z.linkEncode` and `Z.linkDecode`, with UTF-8 and base64url written out by hand so a link means exactly the same thing in a browser and in the headless check. Both ends run through the same canonical form, so the theory check can prove the strong thing rather than a weak one: for a fresh track, all six demos and the heaviest project the app can make, a link comes back byte for byte the same project, and every section of it is then generated at both ends and compared note for note — chords, lead, arp, bass and drums — before every note is checked against the key. It also proves a link is URL-safe, that a bare code opens the same as a hash, that defaults really are left out, that anything which is not a Zinth link is refused rather than half-opened, and that a link written by hand with nonsense in every field still opens as a project that plays in key. Nothing about playback changed, so the WAV and MIDI exports are untouched.

- Six demo songs, one press away. The help sheet — the sheet that opens by itself the first time Zinth is ever opened — now leads with six finished songs, so the first ten seconds of the app are the best ten seconds of it rather than a random roll. **Late Bus Home** is Dorian keys over a dusty break, for a window seat at night. **Night Drive** is A minor, four on the floor and a hook that will not let go. **Lantern Street** is Phrygian dominant over a slow trap kit, all flat second and shadow. **Coin Rush** is square waves, an 808 cowbell and no apologies. **Sunrise Flight** is a house kit under a wide supersaw and a chorus that lifts off. **Glass Observatory** is In-sen, held sevenths and a lot of air between the notes. Each one is a whole project rather than a preset: its own key and scale, tempo, swing, mood, an arrangement of seven to nine sections, a progression for the verses and another for the choruses, a drum pattern written step by step for each part, a hook sitting in the lead lane, a sound for every layer, an EQ setting and a warmth setting. A press loads it through the very same `restore()` that Open uses and starts playback, because the point of a demo is to hear it; <kbd>1</kbd>–<kbd>6</kbd> do the same from the sheet, and because loading one is an ordinary project change it autosaves and undoes like any other — <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>Z</kbd> brings back the track you had before. From the moment it opens there is nothing special about it: the ↺ badge beside the lead lane says the hook is yours, the drum grid holds the written pattern, New track rerolls it, and Export WAV and Export MIDI take it away. The songs live in `src/demos.js` in a form that cannot leave the key rather than one that is carefully checked afterwards: a drum row is sixteen characters, a chord is a degree of the scale's own curated pool, and a hook note is a step, a length and a *scale degree* — 0 the tonic at the bottom of that lane, 7 the tonic an octave up — which `degPitch` resolves to one of the pitch rows the piano roll itself draws, so every note of a demo is a note you could have drawn with the mouse. Section types and lane pitch rows moved into `theory.js` on the way, so the arranger, the demos and the theory check now build a section and a lane from one table. The check covers all six: every field `restore()` reads is there, every section is a type the arranger knows with all five of its layer switches and a legal length, every chord is a real degree of the chord scale and never a diminished one, every drum row is sixteen steps of real velocities with a kick in it, and then the whole song is generated section by section with the demo's own chords, drums and hook in place and every note of every layer — chords, lead, arp, bass — proved to be in the key, with each hook note landing exactly on a pitch row of its own lane. The Sound panel's Kit menu also now follows a project that is opened or restored rather than waiting for the drums tab to be looked at.

- A master strip, with a three-band EQ. The Sound panel has a sixth tab — **master** — and it holds the whole mix rather than a layer. Three bands run down it: **High**, a shelf at 4.2 kHz for the air on top, the hats and the shine on the lead; **Mid**, a peak at 1 kHz for the body, where the chords and the lead sing; and **Low**, a shelf at 160 Hz for the weight underneath. Each runs from a full 9 dB cut, through flat in the middle, to a full 9 dB boost, and its slider fills out from the centre rather than from the left so a cut reads as a cut at a glance; each reads its own gain in decibels, and **Flat** puts all three back at once. Under them sit the **Sidechain pump**, which has moved here from the drums tab because ducking the whole mix under every kick was always a master effect rather than a drum one, and the master fader with the level meter, tied to the volume in the top bar so the two always read the same. The EQ is first in the master chain, ahead of warmth, the section sweep and the limiter, the way a console strip runs into tape. It cannot wreck the mix, and that is proved rather than hoped for: `Z.eqPeakDb` sweeps the audible range for the loudest thing the three bands do together — a pass across 193 points, then a closer look either side of the peak, so it finds the real maximum and not a grid's nearest guess — and the trim after the bands keeps whatever is left inside `Z.EQ.headDb` of headroom. Below that there is no trim at all, so a gentle move is exactly what you asked for; past it the EQ hands the difference straight back, so you can push all three bands to the end and the mix still comes out no more than 3 dB louder than flat. Flat across all three is a true bypass: a band at 0 dB is dropped from the chain entirely, and a biquad at 0 dB has the same numerator and denominator anyway. The bands' magnitude response lives in `theory.js` as the same RBJ coefficients the browser's own filters use, so the theory check measures what the master bus actually does: over every setting of the three bands it proves the EQ never lifts any frequency past its headroom, that the sweep really found the peak the trim is built on, that the trim only ever takes level off, that each band moves its own frequency the way its label says and leaves the far end of the spectrum alone, that more knob is always more decibels, and that flat moves nothing at all. The whole EQ renders in the offline render in `exportWav`, so a WAV is shaped exactly as you heard it; the MIDI export is untouched, because an EQ colours a mix and does not move a note. It lives in `state.eq` in the project snapshot, so it autosaves, undoes, and survives Save and Open — and a project saved before it existed opens flat.

## 2026-09-10

- Five more scales, and a scale menu you can read. The Scale menu now holds eighteen, grouped: the **modes** you already know, an **exotic** shelf, and the **five-note** scales that cannot clash. Five of them are new. **Lydian dominant** is sunlit and slippery, a raised fourth over a flat seventh, the sound of a film cue that will not settle. **Dorian ♭2** is smoky, a flat second at the bottom with a bright sixth above it. **Hungarian minor** has two big leaps in it and is all drama. **Phrygian dominant** is a major chord with a Phrygian second under it — flamenco, and every desert scene ever scored. **Neapolitan minor** is a minor scale under a flat second and a leading note, solemn and cinematic. Every scale, old and new, now carries a `group` so the menu stays readable and a `hint` — one line of plain language about what it sounds like — which becomes the tooltip on its option, on the Scale menu itself and on the key name above the chord cards, so you can find the sound you want by hovering rather than by guessing. The new scales are reachable straight from a mood chip as well as the menu: Dreamy can roll Lydian dominant, Dark can roll Phrygian dominant or Hungarian minor, Otherworldly can roll Dorian ♭2 or Neapolitan minor. Each new scale brings its own pool of progressions, picked by hand rather than generated, because these scales are exactly the ones where stacking thirds blindly goes wrong: Lydian dominant has two diminished triads in it, Dorian ♭2 has two more, Phrygian dominant has two, and a diminished chord sitting under a rolled melody is the one thing in Zinth that can sound like a mistake. So no pool anywhere in the app holds a degree that builds one, and the check now proves that — for every scale, over every pool, as a triad and as a seventh — along with the shape of every scale itself: it starts on its root, it rises, it stays inside the octave, it has at least five notes, any passing tone it declares is really one of its notes, every pool touches the tonic, and every scale either brings a pool or borrows the harmony of one that does. It also rolls a fresh progression in every scale in three keys and proves not one rolled chord comes out diminished. Two chords are named more honestly along the way: a scale with a big leap in it stacks a sixth where the seventh would be, so Hungarian minor's vii and Neapolitan minor's VII now read as m6 and 6 chords with a ⁶ on the card instead of a wrong seventh, and a minor chord under a leading note — harmonic minor's home chord, and Hungarian minor's — reads as mM7 rather than m7. Nothing else changed: every scale is still just a set of steps the generators already knew how to walk, so melodies, arps, basses, the chord box, the play-along keys, the WAV and the MIDI all followed the new scales in without a line of their own.

- Arp modes, octave range and gate. The arp tab of the Sound panel now says what the arp actually plays, not just what it sounds like. **Arp pattern** is the order it walks the chord in: **Up** runs up it and starts again at the bottom, **Down** runs down it, **Up-down** turns around at the top and comes back, **Random** takes a different tone every step and never repeats a figure, **Chord** drops the whole chord at once as block stabs, and **Pattern** repeats one little shape over the chord the way an old hardware arpeggiator does. **Auto**, the seventh choice, is not a mode at all: it means the roll picks one, so the arp's 🎲 in the mixer deals a new figure every time, and it is what a project saved before any of this opens with, playing exactly as it always did. **Arp octaves** decides how far up it reaches — one octave stays put, three sweep up the chord — and **Arp gate** how much of its step each note holds, from a 15 % staccato tick to a 100 % legato run straight into the note after it. Every mood now deals the arp a figure that suits it, from the same roll that picks the key and the tempo: Retro gets a one-octave chip figure with a short gate, Dreamy a three-octave run held almost the whole step, Driving a tight two-octave pulse, Otherworldly random tones and block stabs. A locked arp keeps its figure through a New track, and the layer's 🎲 rolls the pattern, the range and the gate along with the patch. Under all of it the scale lock is unchanged, because none of the three can reach a note that is not there: whatever the mode, the arp is choosing an index into the tones of the chord playing under it, folded into the octave at the bottom of its own lane and stacked as far up as the range asks — so the check proves, over every scale, that every note the arp plays is a tone of that chord, inside the lane, one note to a step (or a whole chord at once in block mode, never more than `Z.ARP.maxBlock` notes, at half the speed of a run), and never long enough to run into the note after it. Block stabs and long gates render in the WAV export and go out in the MIDI, where a note's length is now rounded to the nearest tick because a gated arp note can be a fraction of a step long. The arp's lane in the roll grew from two octaves and a fifth to three and a fifth, so the widest range still draws inside it, and dragging a generated arp note's edge rounds it to whole steps the way a drawn note has always been. All three settings live in the project snapshot, so they autosave, undo and survive Save and Open.
- Bass glide and lead vibrato. The bass tab of the Sound panel has a **Glide** amount now, and the lead tab a **Vibrato** depth with a rate beside it — each control appears only on the layer it belongs to, so the panel says what that layer can actually do. Glide is portamento: the bass starts each note on the pitch of the note before and slides up or down into it, sub oscillator and all, the way a finger slides along a string. It only slides between notes that belong to the same phrase — more than `Z.GLIDE.gap` apart and the bass simply plays the new note, the way a player lifts their hand — and the slide is never longer than 220 ms and never takes more than 45 % of the note it is arriving at, so a fast bass line still reads as notes rather than a smear. Vibrato is the delayed kind: one slow sine into the detune of every oscillator of the voice, held at nothing for the first 280 ms of a note and then faded in over a third of a second, so a sixteenth-note line stays dead straight and a held note comes alive on its own — the way a singer or a string player leans into a long note rather than shaking from the first instant. Depth reads in cents and rate in hertz, from a slow 2.2 Hz lean to a 7.2 Hz shimmer, and a note held on the letter keys sings the same way, so playing along sounds like the track. Both come set gently on a fresh track, both are part of what a mood picks — Dreamy and Otherworldly lean hard into vibrato and a long bass slide, Retro switches both off so a chiptune stays machine-tight — and the 🎲 random patch rolls whichever the layer has. Both bend pitch, so both live under the scale lock: a slide is an exponential ramp in frequency, which is a straight line in semitones, so it crosses the interval evenly, never overshoots either end of it and always arrives exactly on the note that was played; and the vibrato swing is capped at ±26 cents, chosen so that analog drift, the widest chorus voice and a full vibrato all at once still come to under half a semitone. The theory check proves all of it: that a slide lands on its target and stays inside its interval for every pair of scale pitches in the bass register in all 12 keys and every scale, that a note shorter than the onset never gets vibrato at all, that everything that can bend one sounding note together still rounds to the note that was played in every key and scale, and that the bass is the only layer with a glide and the lead the only one with a vibrato. They live in `playNote`, which the offline render calls too, so a WAV slides and sings exactly like the playback; the MIDI export asks your instrument for the same, as CC65 and CC5 portamento on the bass track and GM2 CC76, CC77 and CC78 vibrato depth, rate and delay on the lead. Both are part of the layer's sound in the project snapshot, so they autosave, undo and survive Save and Open — and a project saved before they existed now fills any sound setting it does not carry from the defaults, instead of inheriting whatever the current session happened to have.
- Two more kits and a perc row. The Kit picker has six drum machines now. **House** is punchy and open: a short, hard kick, a crisp snare and hats that stay bright and ring on. **Breaks** is dusty: a low kick under a lowpass, softer hats, and a snare with a dark room tail behind it, so it sounds like it was recorded somewhere rather than assembled. The tail hangs off the drum bus like every other hit, so muting the drums silences it too, and it renders offline in the WAV export. Under the kit, the step grid has a sixth row: **perc**. Every kit names the voice its perc row plays and the row wears that name — the 808 a cowbell, the 909 and Trap a rim, Lo-fi and House a shaker, Breaks a rim — so switching kit re-labels the row and changes what you hear without touching a single hit you placed. The voices are synthesised like the rest of the kit: two detuned squares through a narrow bandpass for the cowbell, a tight band-passed noise snap with a tonal ping for the rim, and bright noise with a hint of an attack for the shaker, so it sounds shaken rather than clicked. The generator uses it the way a producer would: nothing at all below 45 % energy, and above it a sixteenth figure of offbeats or pickups, never on the 1 the kick owns, never on a step the snare or the clap already has, and never louder than `Z.PERC.maxVel` — perc sits behind the backbeat, not in front of it. The last bar's fill keeps its snare roll clean by dropping perc along with the hats. Chill now rolls the Breaks kit and Uplifting the House kit, so both are one mood chip away. A pattern saved before any of this existed has no perc row, so `Z.normDrumPattern` gives every pattern its six rows of sixteen steps in range on the way to playback: an old song opens with an empty perc row and every hit it always had. The row draws as a diamond in the drum lane of the roll, so it reads apart from the hats; it is part of the pattern the grid edits, so it autosaves, undoes and survives Save and Open; and it reaches your DAW as the General MIDI note for whichever voice the kit plays — side stick, maracas or cowbell. The theory check now loads `src/engine.js` too, so the kits themselves are covered: every kit can play every row of the grid, every perc voice is one the engine synthesises and the MIDI export has a GM drum note for, and a generated perc figure is proved to stay off the backbeat, stay quiet, stay inside the loop and stay away from quiet tracks entirely.
- Chorus and warmth. Every synth layer has a **Chorus** amount in the Sound panel now, beside Delay and Reverb, and it sends that layer into a shared stereo chorus: three short delay lines — 11.5, 17.8 and 24.2 ms — each drifting under its own slow LFO and panned to its own place in the field, so a pad spreads out across the stereo image and a lead sounds like more than one player.
