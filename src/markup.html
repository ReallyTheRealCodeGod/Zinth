How Zinth works

Six songs to start from

Press one and it plays. Each is a finished project — its own key and scale, chords, a drum pattern written step by step, a hook in the lead lane and a sound for every layer — and from the moment it opens it is yours: reroll it, draw in it, export it. 1–6 load them from this sheet, and Ctrl+Z brings your own track back.

  1. Press Play. You are hearing a whole song: intro, verses, choruses, a break, a drop. Every note of every layer is locked to one key and scale, so nothing ever sounds off.
  2. Roll until you smile. New track rerolls everything. Lock a layer in the mixer to keep it, hit its dice to reroll just that one. Moods pick scale, tempo and sounds for you.
  3. Pick a scale, get a mood. The Scale menu holds eighteen, grouped so you can read it: the modes you already know, five-note scales that cannot clash, and a shelf of exotic ones — Lydian dominant sunlit and slippery, Dorian ♭2 smoky, Hungarian minor all drama, Phrygian dominant flamenco and desert, Neapolitan minor solemn and cinematic. Hover any of them and it tells you what it sounds like. Whichever you choose, the chords Zinth rolls come from a pool picked by hand for that scale, so there is never a sour chord under the tune, and the pads, the keys and every generated layer move with you.
  4. Play along. Number keys are chords, letter keys are notes, all in key. Sketch chords into the tray and send them to the verse or the chorus.
  5. Shape the chords. Click a chord card above the arrangement to open it: pick another degree, give that one chord its seventh, choose which note sits in the bass, or set how many bars it lasts — I for four bars, then IV and V for two. The progression always fills eight bars, so what no longer fits steps aside. Split a card in two to add a chord, and ↺ Generated brings the rolled progression back. ← → step between chords, Esc closes.
  6. Find the tempo, then count yourself in. Under the tempo slider are three little buttons. Tap takes the tempo from you: tap it in time with the pulse in your head and from the second tap Zinth is already following, four taps settle it — and if you tap half speed or double speed it folds back into range, so whatever you tap it lands on a tempo the song can play. Shift+T taps without reaching for the mouse. Beside it, − and + nudge the tempo one beat a minute at a time, and so do the − and + keys. Then, when you press Rec from a standing start, Count-in gives you one bar of clicks first — four beats counting down where the bar and beat usually are, so you can hear the tempo and come in on the one instead of scrambling at the top of the loop. It is a way into a take, not part of the song: nothing of the count reaches the song, the WAV or the MIDI. Switch it off next to Click if you would rather start straight away.
  7. Let it breathe. Under Swing in the Track panel is Humanize: how much like a player and how little like a machine the track plays. Turn it up and every note gets its own small nudge off the grid and its own change of velocity, so a pattern stops sounding typed in and starts sounding played — except the kick, which never moves and never changes level, so the pulse stays exactly where it is and everything else leans around it. The readout tells you what it is doing at this tempo, in milliseconds. A nudge is always well under half a step, so the rhythm you wrote is still the rhythm you hear, and every one of them comes from the track's own seed rather than a die thrown as it plays — which means a song always plays the same way twice and the WAV and MIDI exports are exactly the performance you heard. Right down is machine-tight, for chiptune and anything else that wants to be a machine; every mood already comes set the way it plays, from Retro dead on the grid to Chill leaning right back off it.
  8. Record your hook. Press Rec, choose whether you are playing into the lead, the arp or the bass, and play the letter keys over the looping section. Your notes replace that generated layer, snapped to the grid. The arp records an octave up and the bass drops into its own register, so a take always sits where that layer sings. I switches layer, and undo is always one press away.
  9. Draw notes in the roll. The amber lead lane, the teal arp lane and the rose bass lane are yours: click an empty spot to add a note, click a note to remove it, drag its right edge for length. Notes snap to the grid and to the key, the bass stays in its own register, edits are undoable, and ↺ beside a lane name (or Clear melody for the lead) brings the generated notes back.
  10. Sweep a section. Pick a section and set Sweep in the inspector: ↗ up starts the whole mix dark and opens it over the section — an intro or a build — and ↘ down closes it over the last bar for an outro or a hand-off into the next part. Intros, pre-choruses and breaks come set to open up, outros to close down. A swept section shows its arrow in the arrangement, and the WAV export sweeps exactly as you heard it.
  11. Fade a section. Beside Sweep is Fade: ◢ in lifts the whole mix out of silence over the section's first two bars, the way a song starts, and ◣ out lets it fall away to silence over its last two, the way a song ends. Outros come set to fade out, so a fresh track already ends gracefully; any section can fade, and the section after a fade-out comes back at full level. A faded section shows its wedge in the arrangement, the WAV export fades exactly as you heard it, and the MIDI export carries the fade as volume automation.
  12. Give it some analog. Each layer in the Sound panel has an Analog drift amount, already on and gentle. It lets that layer's voices wander a few cents in pitch and open their filter a shade differently on every note, the way a real analog synth does, so repeated notes never stack up identically and a held pad breathes. It is measured in cents and capped far under a semitone, so the note itself never changes — turn it right down for machine-tight chiptune, right up for something seasick.
  13. Let it slide and sing. Pick the bass tab in the Sound panel and there is a Glide amount: the bass slides up or down into each note from the one before, the way a finger slides along a string, whenever the two notes are close enough together to belong to one phrase. Pick the lead tab and there is Vibrato and a rate beside it: a note you hold waits a moment and then comes alive, the way a singer leans into a long note, while short notes stay dead straight. Both come set gently, both are in the WAV export, and both are locked to the key — a slide always lands exactly on the note it was heading for, and vibrato is measured in cents, never a semitone.
  14. Tell the arp what to play. Pick the arp tab in the Sound panel and there are three choices that change the notes rather than the tone. Arp pattern is the order it walks the chord in: up, down, up-down, random, Chord for block stabs of the whole chord, or Pattern for a little figure repeating the way an old arpeggiator does — and Auto lets every roll of the arp's 🎲 deal a new one. Arp octaves is how far up it reaches, from one octave sitting still to three sweeping up the chord, and Arp gate how much of its step each note holds: right down is a staccato tick, right up is a legato run straight into the next note. Every mood already deals a figure that suits it — Retro a one-octave chip pattern, Dreamy a long three-octave run — and whatever you choose, every note is a tone of the chord playing underneath, so the arp cannot leave the key.
  15. Widen it and warm it. Each layer has a Chorus amount in the Sound panel: it sends that layer into a stereo chorus — three short delay lines drifting under their own slow LFOs, spread across the field — so a pad spreads out and a lead sounds like more than one player. Up in the top bar, beside the volume, Warmth drives the whole mix into a soft clip and rolls the top end off a shade, the way tape or a valve does: the quiet half comes up, the peaks round over, nothing turns brittle. Turn it right down for a clean digital mix. Neither ever moves a note far enough to change it, both are in the WAV export, and the MIDI asks your instrument for the same chorus.
  16. Mix the whole thing. The last tab in the Sound panel is master, and it holds the mix rather than a layer: a three-band EQ — High for the air on top, Mid for the body where the chords and the lead sing, Low for the weight underneath — each running from a full cut through flat in the middle to a full boost, with Flat to put all three back. Under them sits the Sidechain pump that ducks everything beneath each kick, and the master fader with its meter. The EQ keeps its own headroom: lean on all three bands at once and it quietly takes the difference back, so it can shape the mix but never make it clip. It is in the WAV export exactly as you hear it.
  17. Pick a kit and add some perc. The Sound panel's drums tab has six kits — 808 deep and boomy, 909 punchy, Lo-fi soft and dusty, Trap long-tailed, House punchy with open bright hats, Breaks dusty with a room-y snare. Under the kit, the step grid's last row is perc, and the kit decides what it plays: a cowbell, a rim or a shaker. Busy sections come with a perc figure already, off the backbeat and behind it in level; click the row to write your own. It follows the kit into the WAV export and reaches your DAW as the matching GM percussion note.
  18. Send it to someone. Copy link in the top bar puts the whole song into a link — the key and scale, the tempo, the arrangement, your chords, every note you drew or recorded, the drum grid, the sound of every layer, the EQ and the master. The song travels inside the link, so nothing is uploaded, there is nothing to sign in to and no link ever goes stale; it lands on your clipboard and in the address bar at once, so if copying is not allowed you can still take it from there. Whoever opens it gets the song and it is immediately theirs to change. Opening one never costs you your own track — it is one Ctrl+Z away. A fresh track makes a link of a couple of hundred characters and a whole song a couple of thousand, because everything still sitting at its default is left out; draw every lane full of notes and the link says so, and Save writes a .json file instead.
  19. Shape and leave. Edit the arrangement, kits, the drum grid, patches and effects. Then export WAV or MIDI and finish it anywhere.
