How Zinth works

  1. Press Play. You are hearing a whole song: intro, verses, choruses, a break, a drop. Every note of every layer is locked to one key and scale, so nothing ever sounds off.
  2. Roll until you smile. New track rerolls everything. Lock a layer in the mixer to keep it, hit its dice to reroll just that one. Moods pick scale, tempo and sounds for you.
  3. Play along. Number keys are chords, letter keys are notes, all in key. Sketch chords into the tray and send them to the verse or the chorus.
  4. Shape the chords. Click a chord card above the arrangement to open it: pick another degree, give that one chord its seventh, choose which note sits in the bass, or set how many bars it lasts — I for four bars, then IV and V for two. The progression always fills eight bars, so what no longer fits steps aside. Split a card in two to add a chord, and ↺ Generated brings the rolled progression back. ← → step between chords, Esc closes.
  5. Record your hook. Press Rec, choose whether you are playing into the lead, the arp or the bass, and play the letter keys over the looping section. Your notes replace that generated layer, snapped to the grid. The arp records an octave up and the bass drops into its own register, so a take always sits where that layer sings. I switches layer, and undo is always one press away.
  6. Draw notes in the roll. The amber lead lane, the teal arp lane and the rose bass lane are yours: click an empty spot to add a note, click a note to remove it, drag its right edge for length. Notes snap to the grid and to the key, the bass stays in its own register, edits are undoable, and ↺ beside a lane name (or Clear melody for the lead) brings the generated notes back.
  7. Sweep a section. Pick a section and set Sweep in the inspector: ↗ up starts the whole mix dark and opens it over the section — an intro or a build — and ↘ down closes it over the last bar for an outro or a hand-off into the next part. Intros, pre-choruses and breaks come set to open up, outros to close down. A swept section shows its arrow in the arrangement, and the WAV export sweeps exactly as you heard it.
  8. Fade a section. Beside Sweep is Fade: ◢ in lifts the whole mix out of silence over the section's first two bars, the way a song starts, and ◣ out lets it fall away to silence over its last two, the way a song ends. Outros come set to fade out, so a fresh track already ends gracefully; any section can fade, and the section after a fade-out comes back at full level. A faded section shows its wedge in the arrangement, the WAV export fades exactly as you heard it, and the MIDI export carries the fade as volume automation.
  9. Give it some analog. Each layer in the Sound panel has an Analog drift amount, already on and gentle. It lets that layer's voices wander a few cents in pitch and open their filter a shade differently on every note, the way a real analog synth does, so repeated notes never stack up identically and a held pad breathes. It is measured in cents and capped far under a semitone, so the note itself never changes — turn it right down for machine-tight chiptune, right up for something seasick.
  10. Let it slide and sing. Pick the bass tab in the Sound panel and there is a Glide amount: the bass slides up or down into each note from the one before, the way a finger slides along a string, whenever the two notes are close enough together to belong to one phrase. Pick the lead tab and there is Vibrato and a rate beside it: a note you hold waits a moment and then comes alive, the way a singer leans into a long note, while short notes stay dead straight. Both come set gently, both are in the WAV export, and both are locked to the key — a slide always lands exactly on the note it was heading for, and vibrato is measured in cents, never a semitone.
  11. Tell the arp what to play. Pick the arp tab in the Sound panel and there are three choices that change the notes rather than the tone. Arp pattern is the order it walks the chord in: up, down, up-down, random, Chord for block stabs of the whole chord, or Pattern for a little figure repeating the way an old arpeggiator does — and Auto lets every roll of the arp's 🎲 deal a new one. Arp octaves is how far up it reaches, from one octave sitting still to three sweeping up the chord, and Arp gate how much of its step each note holds: right down is a staccato tick, right up is a legato run straight into the next note. Every mood already deals a figure that suits it — Retro a one-octave chip pattern, Dreamy a long three-octave run — and whatever you choose, every note is a tone of the chord playing underneath, so the arp cannot leave the key.
  12. Widen it and warm it. Each layer has a Chorus amount in the Sound panel: it sends that layer into a stereo chorus — three short delay lines drifting under their own slow LFOs, spread across the field — so a pad spreads out and a lead sounds like more than one player. Up in the top bar, beside the volume, Warmth drives the whole mix into a soft clip and rolls the top end off a shade, the way tape or a valve does: the quiet half comes up, the peaks round over, nothing turns brittle. Turn it right down for a clean digital mix. Neither ever moves a note far enough to change it, both are in the WAV export, and the MIDI asks your instrument for the same chorus.
  13. Pick a kit and add some perc. The Sound panel's drums tab has six kits — 808 deep and boomy, 909 punchy, Lo-fi soft and dusty, Trap long-tailed, House punchy with open bright hats, Breaks dusty with a room-y snare. Under the kit, the step grid's last row is perc, and the kit decides what it plays: a cowbell, a rim or a shaker. Busy sections come with a perc figure already, off the backbeat and behind it in level; click the row to write your own. It follows the kit into the WAV export and reaches your DAW as the matching GM percussion note.
  14. Shape and leave. Edit the arrangement, kits, the drum grid, patches and effects. Then export WAV or MIDI and finish it anywhere.
